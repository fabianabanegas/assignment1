The opening night in Havana felt less like a gig and more like a reunion with an old friend. The room was small, humid and buzzing before the first record even touched the platter. Cami eased in with loose, percussion‑heavy Cuban grooves, letting the dancers find each other in the shadows while the system slowly warmed up.

As the set unfolded, she reached for deeper Afro‑Cuban spiritual pieces, tracks that sounded like they had travelled straight from street processions into the club. Horn stabs and hand drums cut through the smoke as people shouted back at the breaks. By the time the first classic salsa record dropped, the floor was completely full.

The night closed on a run of tender boleros and mid‑tempo soul, a gentle landing after hours of intensity. People stayed to say thank you, to ask about obscure labels, and to tell their own stories about Havana’s musical history.
